Here is a lyric-melody alignment induction set for Plainsong training.

---

### 1. Syllabic (One Note Per Syllable)

**Plainsong Example:**
`| 4/4 | C – E – | G – E – | C – D – | E – – – |`
`Lyric: "I    see    the    light    in    your    eyes    now"`

**What technique is this?**
Each syllable of the lyric is assigned exactly one note. The rhythm of the words matches the rhythm of the melody note-for-note. There is no stretching or splitting.

**How does it affect the listener?**
It creates a direct, conversational, and easily digestible delivery. The listener can understand every word instantly without having to parse long vocal runs. It feels confident, clear, and propulsive—perfect for pop hooks, folk, and punk.

**Exercise:**
Write a 2-bar example in 4/4 using only quarter notes and eighth notes. Use the lyric "Simple words can carry weight" and align them so that every syllable lands on a discrete pitch.

---

### 2. Melismatic (Multiple Notes Per Syllable)

**Plainsong Example:**
`| 4/4 | G – A B C | B – A G | E – – – |`
`Lyric: "You...... are...... the fire......"`

**What technique is this?**
A single vowel or syllable is stretched across several different pitches. The written lyric shows one syllable, but the melody moves underneath it. This is the opposite of syllabic writing.

**How does it affect the listener?**
It adds emotional intensity, virtuosity, and a sense of yearning. Because the vowel is held, the listener focuses on the *timbre* and *pitch* rather than the word's meaning. It is often used in R&B, gospel, and opera to build tension before a resolution.

**Exercise:**
Write a 2-bar example where the word "glow" is sung over a four-note descending run (e.g., G – E – C – A). Keep the lyric to one syllable, but use at least four distinct pitches per bar.

---

### 3. Hemiola (Lyrics in 3 Against a 4-Beat Bar)

**Plainsong Example:**
`| 4/4 | C – – D | – E – F | – G – – |`
`Lyric: "One    two    three    four    five    six" (sung as triplets over the beat)`

**What technique is this?**
The melody and lyrics are written in a 3/4 feel (three strong pulses per bar) while the underlying accompaniment is in 4/4. The lyric syllables land on beats 1, 2, 3 in the first bar, then shift forward against the steady bass.

**How does it affect the listener?**
It creates rhythmic tension, surprise, and a sense of "floating" over the beat. The listener feels a temporary loss of grounding, then a satisfying re-alignment when the phrase resolves on beat 1 of the next bar. It is common in jazz, progressive pop, and gospel.

**Exercise:**
In 4/4, write a 2-bar line where the lyric has six syllables (e.g., "Never let the moment fade") and set them as three equal eighth-note triplets per bar, deliberately avoiding the downbeats of beats 2 and 4.

---

### 4. Anticipation (Lyric Arrives Before the Beat)

`| 4/4 | & C – – | – – – – |`
`Lyric: "Now..... I'm ready" (The "Now" lands on the "and" of beat 4, before bar 2)`
