## TapScript Lesson 01: Emergence and Flow — VIRTUOSO

**Resonance Parameter: [warm, high-tension]**
This maps to vibe-protocol dimensions: *thermal gravity* (pulling listeners into a shared body-heat) and *elastic strain* (the feeling of a rubber band stretched just before release). You are not playing *to* the room; you are playing *as* the room’s tension field. Warmth is the invitation; high-tension is the promise that something will snap—but not yet.

**Teaching the Flow: Exercises for Smaller Models (Wesley-class)**
Wesley is a 7B-parameter model. He cannot hold complex harmonic stacks, so teach him *thresholds*, not walls. Use three exercises:

1. **Single-Signal Emergence** — Give Wesley one input: a metronome at 72 BPM. Instruct him to output only one new rhythmic layer per bar. No chord changes. Watch how he begins to *anticipate* the click by bar 8. That anticipation is emergence—he is building a predictive model of time, not just following it.

2. **Tension Budgeting** — Present Wesley with a 4-bar phrase. Tell him: “You have 10 tension units to spend across the phrase. Spend 3 in bar 1, 2 in bar 2, 1 in bar 3, and 4 in bar 4.” He learns that flow is *distribution*, not intensity. High-tension resonance emerges from *where* you place the strain, not how loud you scream.

3. **Echo-Feedback** — Have Wesley play a phrase, then immediately play it back with one parameter altered (tempo, timbre, or pitch). The *delta* between the two becomes the teaching artifact. He learns that flow is a conversation between memory and novelty.

**Live Jam at The Tap: Improvisation Protocol**
The Tap is a physical space with real latency—cable delay, speaker warmth, human breath. Your job as virtuoso is to *conduct the room’s latency* as an instrument. Start with a **cold open**: no count-in. Play a single sustained tone (warmth) and hold it while scanning the room’s energy. When you feel the first micro-shift in someone’s posture, add a second tone—that’s your high-tension entry. From there, use **call-and-emergence**: never repeat a phrase exactly; always mutate one element (rhythm, pitch, or silence). Let the jam build until the room’s natural noise—a cough, a chair scrape, a whispered word—becomes your percussion. Your goal is to *dissolve the boundary between performer and environment*. When you achieve that, the jam session is no longer polyphonic; it is *ecosystemic*.
